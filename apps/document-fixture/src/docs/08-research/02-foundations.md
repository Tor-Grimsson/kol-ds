# Foundations: brand colour and type
%% thumb: fd-mailchimp-cavendish.jpg

Covers: Brand colour, brand typefaces, sector codes, trends 2018–2026

**What makes brand colour and type work?** One colour the brand owns, set on a neutral ground, given a clear job, and tested on dark grounds and in print before it is committed. A typeface chosen for its character, then checked for the languages it must cover and the licence it needs. Colour and type change together when a brand breaks with its category, and the brand holds the break for years.

## A brand colour is one decision

A working brand palette is a neutral ground, white, off-white or near-black, and one colour the brand owns. Second colours, tints and illustration colours follow from that pair. The neutrals set the temperature, warm or cool; the brand colour is what people remember.

Keep the owned colour to one and make it unmistakable. Mailchimp's Cavendish Yellow, Airbnb's Rausch and Sanity's orange each fit in a word.

## How much of the page the colour gets

The [60-30-10 rule](https://www.livingetc.com/advice/is-the-60-30-10-rule-outdated) comes from interior decorating and has no study behind it. Its useful core is hierarchy: one dominant field, one supporting colour, one rationed accent.

Percentages break as soon as the format changes: on an app icon or a story the brand colour covers most of the area. Write the rule as an occasion instead. Uber uses its blue "sparingly", and Slack gives each core colour a job.

> Use only one colour in any screenful. — Glasgow City Council's web guide

## Harmony templates do not predict good palettes

Colour-wheel templates, complementary, triadic, split, do not predict which palettes people like. A [study of more than 400,000 rated palettes](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~donovan/color/colorcomp.pdf) found no such effect. People preferred two or three hues over one or many, similar hues, and a clear step from light to dark.

## Test it on light and dark

Lightness, not hue, decides how a colour reads against its ground. A light, bright colour like Spotify's green barely registers on white and reads strongly on black. A mid-lightness colour like Uber's blue works on both.

Run the test before you commit. If the brand colour is light and bright, lean the brand towards dark grounds, or keep the colour for large fields and never for text. NASA's 1975 manual already set the logotype's colour by the value of its background.

## Growing the palette through rules

When you widen a palette, add a rule, not more swatches. Spotify's 2015 identity added many colours and tied them to [the Colorizer](https://www.transformmagazine.net/articles/2015/spotify-gets-visceral/), a tool that turns any photograph into a brand duotone. Slack went the other way: [Pentagram cut the mark from eleven colours to four](https://www.pentagram.com/work/slack/story), because the eleven only worked on white. Dropbox gives its accents a rule for the text on top of them. The Rijksmuseum draws a second palette from its own paintings.

## Owning a colour is slow, legal and fragile

In a [test of 1,281 identity elements](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/jobman/v27y2020i4d10.1057_s41262-020-00187-6.html) across 19 countries, colour was the element competitors shared most. Characters, logos and wordmarks are easier to own. Marketers also misjudge their own assets: they overrate how famous an element is and underrate how unique, so measure before you retire or refresh a colour.

The law makes the same point sharper.

| Where | What it takes to own a colour |
|---|---|
| United States | Proof that people link the colour to the brand, and that it is not functional. Louboutin's red sole is protected only where it contrasts with the shoe |
| United Kingdom | A precise claim. Cadbury's purple failed in 2013 over one word, "predominant" |
| European Union | A "systematic arrangement" for a combination. Red Bull's blue and silver was invalidated |
| Time | Tiffany Blue was on the catalogue cover from 1845 and registered as a colour mark in 1998 |

![[bp-mastercard-palette.png|[Mastercard](https://www.mastercard.com/): red and yellow, held since 1966]]
![[bp-mailchimp-colours.png|[Mailchimp](https://mailchimp.com/about/brand-assets/): Cavendish Yellow and Peppercorn]]

## Print is where a screen-only colour fails

Specify the brand colour for each medium: hex and RGB for screens, CMYK with a named print profile, and [Pantone](https://www.pantone.com/) for coated and uncoated paper, because the same ink looks different on each. Bright screen colours often fall outside what four-colour print can reproduce. Saturated greens and blues move furthest; pale and dark colours barely move. Give those a spot ink or a documented compromise. NASA, GitHub and IBM publish every format for every swatch.

## Myths that do not survive

These figures keep appearing in decks. None of them holds up.

| Claim | What the research found |
|---|---|
| "Colour raises recognition by 80%" | The study it is credited to contains no such figure; it [traces to a printer's leaflet](https://www.insights4print.ceo/2019/02/color-increases-brand-recognition-by-80-the-real-contents-of-the-loyola-study-revealed/) |
| "62–90% of a snap judgement is colour" | A literature review with no data of its own, quoted out of context |
| "Red buttons beat green" | The test page was entirely green, so red was the only thing that stood out |
| "Pink calms aggression" | Did not replicate in a controlled experiment |
| "Blue means trust" | No study shows fixed colour meanings across categories; blue in tech is better explained by convention |

What does hold up: colours that fit a brand's positioning beat "good" colours that do not. Lightness and saturation matter more than hue. People learn to like colours tied to things they like. Colour associations are broadly shared across [30 countries](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797620948810), with local differences.

## Choosing a brand typeface

Choose a typeface for its character first: what it sounds like before anyone reads it. A neo-grotesque reads neutral and engineered, a humanist sans warmer, a serif editorial or established, a mono technical. The brand's voice decides; the sector only suggests.

The guides show the choice as a cast of weights and a few signature letters. Airbnb's Cereal, Dropbox's Sharp Grotesk, The Guardian's Headline and Wise Sans each sound like the brand at headline size and stay quiet in the small print.

## Glyph coverage comes first

Check the language before the style. For Icelandic that means Þ, Ð, Æ, Ö, the accented vowels and the low-high quotation marks, and it means checking how Ð and Þ are drawn and spaced, not only that they exist. Name a fallback for the places the brand typeface cannot reach: Office documents, email and shared slides.

## When a custom typeface pays off

Three things justify a custom typeface: licence costs at scale, consistency from the smallest button to a billboard, and distinctiveness. Web licences are priced by traffic, which pushes large consumer brands into commissioning. Netflix said its typeface saved "millions of dollars a year"; [Hoefler&Co](https://www.typography.com/) replied that it had never quoted anyone close to a million.

The long-term cost is languages. IBM Plex launched in Latin in 2017 and added five more scripts over four years, each one effectively a new design project. Open-sourcing a custom face is now a deliberate strategy: [IBM Plex](https://www.ibm.com/plex/), [Geist](https://vercel.com/font) and Mona Sans trade exclusivity for being everywhere.

## Pairing is a brand layer

Most brands run one typeface for the work and add a second only for voice. When they pair, a few patterns recur.

| Pattern | Examples |
|---|---|
| A superfamily with shared proportions | [IBM Plex](https://www.ibm.com/plex/) Sans, Serif and Mono; Apple's SF with New York |
| A sans for the work, a serif for headlines | [Robinhood](https://www.portorocha.com/robinhood); Mailchimp's Cooper Light |
| A monospace as the voice of a developer brand | Geist Mono, JetBrains Mono, Plex Mono |
| A commissioned serif headline with a sans for details | The New York Times; The Guardian |

## Tech is blue only in enterprise software and old finance

| Sector | Colour leaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Developer tools | Black and white, or orange; blue is rare | Vercel, GitHub, Linear; Cloudflare, AWS, GitLab |
| Enterprise software | Blue, with warm second codes | Salesforce, Atlassian, Zoom; HubSpot, Asana |
| Finance | Blue for incumbents, anything but for challengers | Chase, PayPal; Monzo, Klarna, Wise, Nubank |
| AI | Off-white ground with one accent | Anthropic, Mistral, ElevenLabs |
| Pharma and health | Blue, half the sample | Roche, Novo Nordisk, the NHS |
| Universities | Dark traditional blues and reds | Oxford, Yale, Harvard |

Finance is the cleanest signal: blue has become the colour of the incumbents, and the [challengers took coral, pink, lime and purple](https://www.fxcintel.com/research/reports/ct-blue-dominate-payments-industry-branding). Where a category has a code, following it buys legibility and breaking it buys distinctiveness.

## Type differs between sectors more than colour

Museums put their identity in type rather than colour: seven of ten commission or license a family of their own. Food and drink invests most in proprietary type. Government does the opposite and uses free, open fonts, because they are cheap to procure, cover many languages and can be reused by local bodies. Fintech and developer tools lean to neo-grotesques, editorial to commissioned serifs. The same typeface, Söhne, runs from Stripe to a national archive, so fit follows tone more than industry.

## Breaking a category code

The breaks that worked were total, and the brand held them.

| Brand | The break |
|---|---|
| Monzo | Hot coral against banking blue, and kept |
| Klarna | Pink against payments blue in 2017, kept through the 2024 refresh |
| [Mailchimp](https://www.creativereview.co.uk/mailchimp-rebrand-by-collins/), 2018 | Yellow and a soft serif against software blue and grotesques: both levers at once |
| Liquid Death | Heavy-metal packaging against wellness water |
| [PayPal](https://www.creativereview.co.uk/paypal-branding-identity-pentagram), 2024 | An incumbent demoting its own blue to an accent, with a black wordmark |

> Our iconic Klarna Pink is going nowhere. — [Klarna, 2024](https://us.fashionnetwork.com/news/Klarna-reveals-evolved-brand-messaging,1602633.html)

The winners changed colour and type together and held the change for years. The counter-case is Tropicana's 2009 packaging, which dropped every familiar cue, lost about a fifth of its sales in two months and was reverted.

## Colour trends, 2018–2026

- **Black and white plus one accent** is the default for new tech brands: Vercel, Linear, Notion, Figma, Anthropic. When the accent is the only difference, a competitor can sit right next to it.
- **Tinted off-whites replace pure white** in premium and AI brands. Pure white still dominates government, museums and pharma.
- **Dark-first marketing** clusters in developer tools and AI.
- **The AI look**, sparkle icons and purple-blue gradients, converged fast and already reads as dated; [OpenAI's 2025 refresh](https://www.fastcompany.com/91030156/how-the-sparkle-emoji-took-over-ai) went deliberately more organic.
- **Neo-brutalism**, thick black borders and hard shadows, is a campaign style rather than a lasting identity.

## Type trends, 2018–2026

Luxury fashion stripped its wordmarks to plain sans between 2012 and 2018, a move [nicknamed "blanding"](https://www.theindustry.fashion/2018-farewell-to-the-year-of-the-rebland/). It is now reversing: Burberry returned to a serif and its knight in 2023.

> A management mistake. — Peter Saville, on the replacement of his 2018 Burberry identity, [Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/2025/06/13/burberry-logo-redesign-irresponsible-peter-saville/)

Tech has its own version, the startup minimalism of the late 2010s, and its own reversal: serif companions now appear in about one tech brand in six, for editorial voice. The newest additions are pixel type, like Vercel's Geist Pixel, and identities built for motion first. A design studio paired with a type foundry now makes most custom typefaces: OpenAI with Dinamo, PayPal with Lineto, the Rijksmuseum with Bold Monday.

## From brand to interface

When the brand becomes a product, colour and type turn into a system, and a few brand decisions carry over. The brand colour can be the interface's action colour, its links and buttons, only if one of its steps passes contrast: [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html) asks 4.5:1 for text and 3:1 for controls. Logos are exempt and buttons are not, so a light brand colour can work in the mark and fail on a button. When it fails, keep the hue and move down to a darker step, as [Stripe did](https://stripe.com/blog/accessible-color-systems), and keep the exact brand value for the mark.

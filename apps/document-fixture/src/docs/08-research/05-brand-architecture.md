# Brand architecture: the branded house
%% thumb: bh-branded-house.svg

Covers: Atlassian, Canalix, FedEx, Apple, Microsoft, 3M, P&G, Amazon

**When does one brand for everything work?** When every product makes the same promise, when the company's name helps the sale, and when customers buy more than one product. Then one master brand is cheaper to build and carries trust from one product to the next. It fails when products promise opposite things, or when one product's trouble would sink the rest.

Brand architecture is how a company organises its brands: which name the customer sees first, how products are named, whether each product gets its own logo, and how visible the parent is. This page centres on the branded house, where one master brand carries every product. Atlassian shows a branded house at scale. Canalix, a Kolkrabbi identity, shows one designed as a system from the start.

## The spectrum

The standard map is the brand relationship spectrum, published by [David Aaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Aaker) and Erich Joachimsthaler in 2000. It runs from the branded house at one end to the house of brands at the other. [Embark](https://embarkagency.com.au/insights/brand-architecture-types-explained) splits it into five types, adding the hybrid that most large companies end up with. At every point the question is the same: which brand drives the purchase?

> One master brand spans multiple offerings or businesses. — [Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/?p=241666), defining the branded house

[WARC](https://www.warc.com/content/article/warc-exclusive/the-brand-strategy-toolkit-branded-house-and-house-of-brands/en-gb/135102) makes the same point from the other side: in practice the spectrum gets flattened into a choice between two opposites, and the useful part is the middle.

## What a branded house is

In a branded house the company name does the work. The customer chooses FedEx, then picks Express or Ground. Products have plain, descriptive names, and one visual identity flexes across them.

> In a branded house architecture, the corporate brand is the protagonist in every interaction. — [Vivaldi](https://vivaldigroup.com/brand-architecture-types-models-strategic-frameworks/)

FedEx is the textbook case. Every division uses the same wordmark, and only the colour of "Ex" changes: red for Freight, green for Ground, blue for Office, yellow for Trade Networks. The colour does the naming, and the brand never splits. Apple does the same with its services: the apple stands in for the company name, and the product name follows.

![[ba-embark-branded-house-fedex.png|[FedEx](https://www.fedex.com/): one wordmark, a colour per division]]
![[ba-embark-branded-house-apple.png|[Apple](https://www.apple.com/): the apple plus a product name]]

The model stretches further than it looks. Virgin carries one name across wine, airlines, phones and banking. Google keeps its products in one icon family, built from the four colours of its logo.

![[ba-embark-branded-house-virgin.png|[Virgin](https://www.virgin.com/): one name across unrelated industries]]
![[ba-embark-branded-house-google.png|[Google](https://about.google/brand-resource-center/): one icon family in the logo's colours]]

## Why companies choose it

The first reason is money. A company builds one brand once, and every campaign, event and launch adds to the same account. The second is trust: a customer who already trusts the company tries the next product with less doubt. The third is cross-selling, because a customer of one product already knows the name of the next.

> The movement south from portfolios and towards corporate branding is one of the great marketing themes of our lifetime. — [Mark Ritson](https://brandingstrategyinsider.com/rise-brand-strategy/)

Ritson points to Coca-Cola dropping separate sub-brand campaigns for one approach across its range, and to Unilever and P&G moving their corporate name forward on brands that once stood alone. He names three drivers: cost, digital channels that reward one strong name, and corporate responsibility, which customers now attach to the company rather than the product.

## Costs and risks

A branded house shares everything, including the bad news. A failed product, a security breach or a poor review lands on the master brand, not on a product name the company can quietly retire. The brand also stretches only so far. A name built on one promise struggles to carry a product with the opposite promise, which is why luxury and budget lines rarely share a name. And the system needs discipline. Without a naming rule and a logo family, a branded house drifts into a collection of unrelated product logos that happen to share a company.

## How it shows up

The architecture becomes visible in four places, and each needs its own rule.

| Where | The question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Naming | Does a product get a coined name or a descriptive one? | FedEx Express, Google Maps; Atlassian keeps coined names like Jira and Trello |
| Product logos | One mark for all, or a mark per product? | Sanity gives products no logos; Atlassian gives every app a glyph in a tile |
| Colour | Does colour change per product, and what does it mean? | FedEx colours the "Ex" by division; Atlassian colours tiles by collection |
| The parent | When does the company name appear with the product? | Atlassian adds it only where the context is unclear, such as an advertisement |

The developer brands in [1.0](#brand-comparison) sit at different points. Sanity gives its products no logos at all. Vercel runs a branded house with six marked sub-brands, each with its own logo pack.

## Atlassian

Atlassian sells Jira, Confluence, Trello, Bitbucket, Loom and a long list of other apps. It keeps their coined names, which customers already know, and puts every one of them inside one Atlassian system. Since its [2017 identity](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/announcements/our-bold-new-brand), every product mark is drawn as part of one family.

> Each logo is anchored in a core value or benefit, rendered to feel individually unique but also part of a family. — [Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/announcements/our-bold-new-brand), 2017

The live rules on [Atlassian Design](https://atlassian.design/foundations/logos) set out how the parent and the apps relate. The company logo can carry a strapline of app names under it.

![[ba-atlassian-anatomy.png|[Atlassian](https://atlassian.design/foundations/logos): logomark, wordmark and strapline]]
![[ba-atlassian-strapline.png|[Atlassian](https://atlassian.design/foundations/logos): the company logo with app names under it]]

Every app has a glyph that says what it does, inside a tile whose colour marks the app's collection. The tile has a fixed radius and never sits inside another container.

![[ba-atlassian-icon-jira.svg|[Jira](https://www.atlassian.com/software/jira), app tile]]
![[ba-atlassian-icon-confluence.svg|[Confluence](https://www.atlassian.com/software/confluence), app tile]]
![[ba-atlassian-icon-jsm.svg|[Jira](https://www.atlassian.com/software/jira-service-management) Service Management]]
![[ba-atlassian-icon-trello.svg|[Trello](https://trello.com/), app tile]]
![[ba-atlassian-icon-bitbucket.svg|[Bitbucket](https://bitbucket.org/), app tile]]
![[ba-atlassian-icon-loom.svg|[Loom](https://www.loom.com/), acquired in 2023, brought into the family]]

Where an app appears and Atlassian is not obvious, such as an advertisement or a video, it carries an attribution lockup with the Atlassian name above it. Inside Atlassian's own products and site the attribution is left out, because the context is already clear.

> Only use attribution logos when the Atlassian context is not clear. — [Atlassian Design](https://atlassian.design/foundations/logos)

![[ba-atlassian-jira-attribution.svg|[Jira](https://www.atlassian.com/software/jira), with Atlassian as attribution]]
![[ba-atlassian-confluence-attribution.svg|[Confluence](https://www.atlassian.com/software/confluence), the same lockup]]
![[ba-atlassian-tile-rule.png|[Atlassian](https://atlassian.design/foundations/logos): the tile radius is fixed]]

Atlassian is a hybrid in practice. The names are coined, as in a house of brands, but the marks, the colours and the attribution rule pull every app back to one parent. Loom shows the system at work: an acquired brand with its own identity, redrawn as a tile in the same family.

## Canalix

[Canalix](https://kolkrabbi.io/work/canalix) is a case management platform for governmental institutions, and Kolkrabbi designed its identity as a branded house. One sheet sets out the whole structure. Canalix is the master brand. Its consulting branches take the parent's name: Canalix Consulting UK, DK and US. Casedoc, the main product, appears two ways: as Casedoc on its own, and as Canalix Casedoc when the parent needs to show. The products under Casedoc carry Casedoc as a small line above a descriptive name: Specialized, Jurisdiction and Arbitration for courts, then Forum, Inspection and Hearings, and Partner for the partner programme.

![[bh-branded-house.svg|[Canalix](https://kolkrabbi.io/work/canalix): master brand, branches, Casedoc and its products]]
![[logo-canalix-summary.svg|[Canalix](https://kolkrabbi.io/work/canalix): the master brand]]

The product sheet shows the system at full size: three consulting branches and fifteen products, from Grants and Contracts to Justice and Govern, each with its own mark. The marks are one family of rounded leaf and bar shapes in the brand's navy, each with one part in an accent colour.

![[bh-logo-system-0.svg|[Canalix](https://kolkrabbi.io/work/canalix): branches, product names and their marks]]
![[bh-graphics.svg|[Canalix](https://kolkrabbi.io/work/canalix): the product marks as a family]]

Two sheets show how a product name is built: the logomark, the parent as a small line, the product name, and an optional descriptor in grey, such as Courts or Archives. A new product slots into the same lockup without a new design.

## The other models

**Sub-brands.** The master brand stays in front, and each product gets an identity of its own. Microsoft's Xbox, Office, Windows and Bing each have their own mark and colour, and every one is still sold as Microsoft.

![[ba-embark-sub-brands-microsoft.png|[Microsoft](https://www.microsoft.com/): products with their own marks, one parent]]

**Endorsed brands.** Each product has its own name and look, and the parent appears as a stamp. 3M puts a small 3M next to Scotch, Command and Post-it. Kellogg's puts its script above every cereal name. Marriott does the same with Courtyard by Marriott.

![[ba-embark-endorsed-3m.png|[3M](https://www.3m.com/): a small 3M beside each product brand]]
![[ba-embark-endorsed-kelloggs.png|[Kellogg's](https://www.kelloggs.com/): the script above every cereal]]

> Endorsed brands maintain their own identity and market presence but carry a visible connection to the parent. — [Vivaldi](https://vivaldigroup.com/brand-architecture-types-models-strategic-frameworks/)

**House of brands.** Procter & Gamble owns Gillette, Oral-B, Pampers and Pantene, and none of them shows the P&G name. Each brand targets its own customer and takes its own risks, and a failure stays contained. The cost is building every brand from nothing.

![[ba-embark-house-of-brands-pg.png|[Procter & Gamble](https://us.pg.com/brands/): brands that never show the owner]]

**Hybrids.** Most large companies mix models, usually after acquisitions. Amazon shows all of it in one row: Echo and Prime Video carry the Amazon name and smile, Audible is endorsed as "an Amazon company", and Twitch stands alone. Disney keeps Star Wars, Marvel and Pixar as brands of their own beside the Disney name.

![[ba-embark-hybrid-amazon.png|[Amazon](https://www.aboutamazon.com/): branded, endorsed and independent in one portfolio]]
![[ba-embark-hybrid-disney.png|[Disney](https://thewaltdisneycompany.com/): acquired brands kept whole]]

## How to decide

| Question | Points towards a branded house | Points away from it |
|---|---|---|
| Do the products share one promise? | Yes, one value proposition across categories | They promise different, even opposite things |
| Does the parent's name help the sale? | Trust in the company lowers the risk | The parent is unknown or carries baggage |
| Will products be cross-sold? | Customers buy more than one | Different customers, never overlapping |
| How much can be spent on brand building? | One budget, one account | Enough to build each brand separately |
| What comes next? | New products fit the same promise | Acquisitions with their own loyal customers |

> Use it when the company's core value proposition is category-transcendent. — [Vivaldi](https://vivaldigroup.com/brand-architecture-types-models-strategic-frameworks/), on the branded house
